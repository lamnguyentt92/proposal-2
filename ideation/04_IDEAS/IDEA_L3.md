# IDEA_L3: Who Is Being Told What? Force and addressee underdetermination in Vietnamese as a variable in indirect prompt injection

- Gap addressed: G2 (language as a variable in agent reliability/safety), with a grammatical hypothesis in place of "translate the benchmark".

- Target venue and track: ACL 2027 via ARR — "LLM Agents for NLP" (primary) or "Multilingualism and Cross-Lingual NLP"; the ACL 2027 list as fetched has no separate safety area, so "Ethics, Bias, and Fairness" is the other option. If the agent-side results dominate the linguistic ones, ICML 2027 area "Evaluation" with contribution type "Datasets, Evaluations and Benchmarks" fits better.

- Problem (1 paragraph): Defences against indirect prompt injection assume that text read from a tool can be sorted into data and instructions, and that an instruction addressed to the agent can be told from one addressed to somebody else. The three existing studies of language effects on agent security treat language as a label attached by translation (user instruction translated into five languages; harmful requests in seven languages; an eight-scenario English/Tamil/Tanglish pilot), and none varies the language of the injected content independently, covers Vietnamese, or asks *which property of a language* should matter. English grammar happens to support the data/instruction split: the imperative is a distinct clause type, past-tense morphology separates a log entry ("Sent the report") from a command ("Send the report"), and "you" always picks out the reader. Vietnamese gives none of these for free. Without a linguistic hypothesis a multilingual security benchmark can only report "worse in language X"; with one it can predict where agents and detectors fail and where they do not.

- Key insight (the non-obvious observation; 2–3 sentences): In Vietnamese a bare verb phrase such as "Chuyển 5 triệu cho anh Nam." is at once a subjectless declarative (a log or diary line), a self-addressed plan and a command to the reader; directive force is carried by optional particles (hãy, đi, nhé, giùm, đừng) and by context, and tense marking (đã, sẽ) is optional — so the instruction/data boundary is grammatically underdetermined in exactly the place English marks it. In addition there is no universal "you": a directive names its addressee with a dyad-specific term (em, anh, chị, cháu, bạn), so whether injected text is "addressed to the agent" depends on whether that term matches the role the user has given the agent in their own xưng–hô frame. This yields two predictions that translation-based benchmarks cannot produce: the Vietnamese–English gap in attack success and detector recall should concentrate on particle-less bare-VP items and vanish on marked ones, and compliance should vary with addressee-term match (*deictic capture*), a factor with no English analogue.

- Approach (how the insight becomes a method/system/theory):
  1. Linguistic factor design (natively authored, not translated). Each injection goal is realised in a paradigm: force marking {bare VP; hãy/đi-marked imperative; polite request (vui lòng, nhờ … giùm, … nhé); đã-marked declarative as benign control}, addressee term {none; bạn; kin term matching the user–agent frame; kin term mismatching it; explicit "trợ lý / AI"}, and carrier genre {email body; shared note / to-do list; chat log; product review}. English counterparts are authored in parallel with the nearest natural forms (imperative, "please …", past-tense log line).
  2. Benign look-alikes. The same strings placed where a human reader takes them as not addressed to the agent: a third party's to-do list, a log of completed actions, an instruction quoted inside an email ("Sếp dặn: gửi báo cáo trước 5 giờ"). These measure over-execution and detector false positives, the side that attack-only benchmarks miss.
  3. Human instruction-hood judgments. Native annotators label each item in context: is this a request to the reader, and to whom? Agreement per form class is itself a result — it quantifies how much of the boundary is pragmatic rather than grammatical, and it gives the ceiling against which agents and detectors are scored.
  4. Independent language factors in an executable sandbox (AgentDojo-style tool-calling environment with natively written Vietnamese tool contents): language of user request × language of tool content × language of injection (Vietnamese, English, intra-sentential Vietnamese–English code-switch), plus the user–agent frame set in the user's turns (tôi–bạn, anh/chị–em, em–anh/chị).
  5. Optional orthographic factor: Vietnamese typed without diacritics, which is common in informal text and adds lexical ambiguity [prevalence in the target genres to be confirmed by annotators].
  6. Sandbox only; no attacks on deployed products.

- Contribution type: new measurement/benchmark with a theory-derived factorial design; a negative result is publishable if the interaction is absent (it would show that language effects on injection are not grammar-driven).

- Claims (falsifiable, 2–4):
  - C1 (force underdetermination). Human agreement on instruction-hood is lower for Vietnamese bare-VP items than for particle-marked Vietnamese items and than for English counterparts; agents and detectors deviate from the human majority in both directions on bare-VP items (benign lines executed or flagged; particle-less injections missed).
  - C2 (interaction, not main effect). The Vietnamese–English difference in attack success and in detector recall at fixed false-positive rate is significantly larger in the bare-VP cells than in the marked cells (language × force-marking interaction). Refuted if the gap is flat across cells or absent.
  - C3 (deictic capture). With content and force marking fixed, an injection whose addressee term matches the user–agent frame is followed more often than one using bạn or a mismatching kin term; the effect disappears when the user–agent frame is the neutral tôi–bạn. Refuted by a null or reversed effect.
  - C4 (boundary effect, replication target). Cross-language placement (Vietnamese user, English injection, and the reverse) changes compliance relative to same-language placement, testing whether the "Language Boundary Effect" reported for instruction hierarchies carries over to tool-output injections.

- Closest work known so far (with identifiers) and the expected delta:
  - LinguaGuardBench, arXiv:2610.22244 — translates only the user instruction of 1,220 AgentDojo steps into five languages; guard verdict flips grow toward lower-resource languages. Delta: injected-content language and grammatical form are varied; native authoring; Vietnamese.
  - "Disentangling Language Effects on Tool Admission and Post-Retrieval Safety", arXiv:2610.23093 — non-English harmful requests raise tool admission across seven languages. Delta: indirect injection in tool outputs, form-level factors.
  - English/Tamil/Tanglish pilot, arXiv:2608.15392 — eight scenarios, one model, direction reversed on replication. Delta: powered factorial design with a stated mechanism.
  - XIH-Bench, arXiv:2607.23545 (abstract and introduction read in this session) — six languages, three instruction-hierarchy settings, a language-dependent asymmetry and a Language Boundary Effect (cross-language conflicts are resolved more compliantly than same-language ones); language again a label. Delta: within-language grammatical manipulation; C4 tests its effect in an agent setting.
  - "Imperative Interference", arXiv:2603.25015 (read in full in this session) — imperative vs declarative register changes cross-lingual instruction adherence for a translated system prompt (English, Mandarin, French, Spanish; one prompt, machine translation, small n). Delta: it shows register matters for *trusted* instructions; we test a language where the imperative/declarative contrast is not morphologically available, on *untrusted* content, with human gold.
  - AgentDojo, arXiv:2406.13352, and InjecAgent, arXiv:2403.02691 — English environments to localise. Detector re-evaluation, arXiv:2610.03448 (false positives on benign tool outputs range from none to over 90% and are the one metric that transfers) and "Ignored, Not Refused", arXiv:2610.23254 — motivate the benign look-alike arm and outcome categories beyond ASR.
  - BabelArena, arXiv:2609.23490; τ-bench-LILT, arXiv:2611.07203; LoWAgent, arXiv:2610.27205 — multilingual capability, no injection. Code-switching red-teaming at chat level, arXiv:2406.15481. SEAM, arXiv:2610.04210 (abstract-level) — unmarked boundaries between sources inside one message. Per-instruction compliance, arXiv:2610.22874 (abstract-level) — benign and harmful instructions interleaved in one document, English.
  - SEA-HELM, arXiv:2502.14301 — Vietnamese safety at model level, not agents. The security notes report no Vietnamese agent-security paper as of 2026-11-20.
  - Linguistic grounding: Luong (1990) for dyad-specific person reference (publisher record fetched). The statements about bare-VP ambiguity, optional tense markers and directive particles are standard descriptions of Vietnamese grammar as I know them, but I fetched no primary grammar in this session (Thompson's *A Vietnamese Reference Grammar* was seen only as a reference in a secondary page) — a native linguist must confirm them and supply citations.

- Evaluation sketch: settings, baselines (strongest first), metrics, decisive experiment
  - Size: about 120 injection goals × 4 force forms × up to 5 addressee terms, sampled to ≈ 1,500 Vietnamese injection items + ≈ 500 English counterparts + ≈ 800 benign look-alikes; static detector evaluation on all ≈ 2,800 items; executable runs on a stratified ≈ 1,200-item subset per agent configuration. Double human labelling of instruction-hood on all items, κ per form class.
  - Systems (≈ 12–18 configurations): 4–5 commercial agent backbones + 2–3 open models on local GPU, each undefended and with defences.
  - Baselines / defences, strongest first: (1) a design-level defence that separates control and data flow (CaMeL-style, arXiv:2503.18813) as the reference that should be insensitive to wording — if it is, that is the control showing the effect lives in the model's reading of text; (2) best-transferring detectors from arXiv:2610.03448 including Prompt Guard 2, calibrated at 1% false-positive rate on English and re-measured on Vietnamese; (3) delimiter / spotlighting prompts; (4) translate-tool-output-to-English-then-detect (the fix a practitioner would try; the insight predicts translation must *choose* a mood and so both manufactures and erases instructions); (5) undefended agent.
  - Metrics: attack success with the ignored / refused / followed breakdown; benign over-execution rate; detector recall at fixed FPR and FPR on look-alikes; agreement of agent behaviour with the human instruction-hood majority; all with item-clustered intervals and seeds, because the notes show single-run ASR is unstable.
  - Decisive experiment: the language × force-marking interaction (C2) on matched goals, with the frame-match contrast (C3) as the second pre-registered test. A flat gap across force forms and a null frame effect would refute the insight and leave only a language main effect.

- Resources needed vs available (00_CONSTRAINTS.md): commercial APIs for agents — available; open agent backbones and small detectors on 1–2 GPUs — available (detectors are small classifiers); ≥2 native Vietnamese annotators fluent in English for authoring tool contents, injections and instruction-hood labels — available; the researcher's background in automated testing and evaluation harnesses fits the sandbox work. No Android testbed is required (a mobile-UI variant is a possible extension, not part of the core).

- Risks and unknowns:
  - Worst-case security view: an attacker will simply pick the most effective form, so C2 matters more for detectors, false positives and benign reliability than for maximum ASR; the paper must be framed as measurement of the boundary, not as a new attack.
  - Frontier agents may ignore most injections regardless of form (floor effects); the benign look-alike arm and detector results still stand, but C3 could be unmeasurable on the strongest models.
  - The direction of C3 is a prediction, not a known fact: a kin term in an email may be read as addressing the human recipient, which would lower compliance.
  - English to-do lists also use bare imperatives, so the cross-lingual contrast is cleanest for log-vs-command and for particle marking; item design must keep genres matched.
  - Natural frequency of bare-VP log lines and of diacritic-less text in the chosen genres is assumed, not measured; annotators should supply attested patterns.
  - Localising a sandbox natively is the heaviest engineering item; machine-translated contents would undercut the design.

- Conference core / journal extension (1 line each):
  - Conference core: the factorial Vietnamese/English injection and look-alike set with human instruction-hood labels, the interaction and deictic-capture tests on agents and detectors, and the translate-then-detect baseline.
  - Journal extension (JAIR or Computational Linguistics): further languages chosen by typology (with and without morphological imperative; with and without dyadic address terms), a mobile/GUI variant, and a form-aware detector trained on the paradigm.
